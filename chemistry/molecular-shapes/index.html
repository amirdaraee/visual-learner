Why molecules bend

Electron groups around an atom repel each other and spread out as far as they can. Lone pairs push harder than bonds, and that is what bends a molecule.

Why molecules bend

This is the VSEPR model (valence shell electron pair repulsion). Count the electron groups around a central atom, and they arrange themselves as far apart as possible. A group is a lone pair or a whole bond, so a double or triple bond counts once. The shape name describes where the atoms end up.

1 · Groups

Two to six groups, all bonding. The angle between neighbours shrinks as groups are added: 180°, 120°, ~109.5°, then 90° (with 120° too for five).

2 · Lone pairs

Swap bonds for lone pairs in AXnEm notation. Lone pairs are drawn as soft lobes. Greyed-out buttons are combinations this page does not model.

3 · Real molecules

Eight real molecules loaded into the model, with their measured angles. Approximate values carry a ~.

Limits

VSEPR predicts the arrangement, not energies, and it is less reliable for transition metal compounds. The model is drawn at the ideal angles, so it does not show the small squeeze that lone pairs cause. Atom sizes, colours and bond lengths are schematic and not to scale.
