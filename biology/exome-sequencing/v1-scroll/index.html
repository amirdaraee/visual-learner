Clinical genetics, step by step

Reading the Exome

An exome test reads the roughly 1 to 2 percent of your DNA that codes for proteins, then searches it for the one change that explains a patient's disease. Scroll through the pipeline and play with each stage.

Educational overview. Numbers are typical orders of magnitude and vary by lab, kit and patient. It is not medical advice, and real variant interpretation is done by clinical geneticists and laboratory specialists.

Stage 1 · Why the exome

Read 1% of the genome to find most of the answers

Your genome has about 3.1 billion base pairs. Around 20,000 genes spell out proteins, and the stretches that carry that code, called exons, add up to only 30 to 60 million bases. That slice is the exome.

Early studies estimated that about 85% of the large-effect disease variants known at the time sat in exons. Part of that is bias, because exons were what people could read, but the exome is still where a rare-disease search has the best odds per dollar. The same sequencer budget buys roughly 100× depth on the exome or about 30× on the whole genome.

Stage 2 · Wet lab

Capture the exons, wash away the rest

DNA from blood or saliva is shredded into fragments of about 150 to 300 bases and tagged with adapters. Then comes the trick that defines the method: hybrid capture. Biotin-labelled bait strands, designed to match every exon, grab their matching fragments. Magnetic beads grab the baits and a magnet holds them while everything else is washed off.

  1. Shear the DNA into short fragments and add adapters.
  2. Baits find and bind the exon fragments, beads pull them in.
  3. Magnet holds the beads while off-target DNA is washed away.
  4. Elute, amplify by PCR, load onto the sequencer.

The sequencer then reads each fragment from both ends, typically 2 × 100 to 150 bases, producing tens of millions of short reads in FASTQ files.

Stage 3 · Bioinformatics

Align the reads and spot where they disagree

Each read is matched to its place on the reference genome (GRCh38) with an aligner such as BWA-MEM. Duplicates are marked and base qualities are recalibrated, producing a BAM or CRAM file. A variant caller such as GATK HaplotypeCaller or DeepVariant then walks along every position and asks whether the reads disagree with the reference in a consistent way.

The share of reads carrying the alternate base (the variant allele fraction) tells the story. Switch the scenario and watch the pileup.

Isolated mismatches elsewhere in the pileup are random sequencing errors. Callers weigh base quality, mapping quality, strand balance and depth to tell them apart. The result is a VCF file listing every site where this person differs from the reference, usually around 25,000 variants per exome.

Stage 4 · Filtering

From 25,000 variants to one suspect

Most variants are harmless. The analysis is a series of gates, each throwing out variants that cannot explain the disease. Hover a gate to light it up in the scene. The dots are schematic, the counts are typical.

    Filters are tuned to the suspected inheritance, and they are rerun with looser settings when the first pass finds nothing. A variant that is too common to cause a rare disease is the most powerful single exclusion in the whole process.

    Stage 5 · Family data

    Use the parents to read the inheritance

    Sequencing the patient together with both parents (a trio) turns an ambiguous list into a sharp one. The genotypes of mother and father show whether a variant is new, inherited, or one half of a pair. Pick a pattern and watch where the variant sits in each person.

    Pink and amber bands are two different variants inside the same gene (the teal zone). Trios typically push the diagnostic yield toward the upper end of the 25 to 40% range reported for exome sequencing in rare disease, mainly because de novo variants stand out immediately.

    Stage 6 · Interpretation

    Weigh the evidence on the ACMG scale

    The surviving candidates are classified with the ACMG/AMP guidelines into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign and benign. Each piece of evidence has a code and a weight. Combining rules turn the pile into a verdict.

    What the exome misses

    • Repeat expansions such as Huntington disease and Fragile X.
    • Large deletions and duplications (copy number variants), which are partly detectable from read depth.
    • Deep intronic and regulatory variants that disturb splicing or expression.
    • Low-level mosaic variants present in only a fraction of cells.
    • GC-rich exons and genes with near-identical pseudogenes, where reads align poorly.

    Diagnostic yield and reanalysis

    About 25 to 40% of rare-disease patients get a molecular diagnosis from the first analysis. The rest are not necessarily negative. A new disease gene may be published, a VUS may be reclassified, or a better caller may be available. Reanalysing the same data every year or two often adds roughly another 10% of diagnoses.

    Reading a variant name

    Variants follow HGVS notation. This one is a well-known hotspot in TP53:

    NM_000546.6:c.817C>T p.(Arg273Cys)

    Transcript, then the cDNA change (base 817, C to T), then the protein change (arginine 273 becomes cysteine).

    Zygosity, penetrance, phasing

    • Genotype 0/1 or 1/1. One or both copies carry the variant.
    • Penetrance. Not everyone with a pathogenic variant gets ill.
    • Phasing. Two variants in a gene matter differently on the same copy or on opposite copies. Parents resolve it.
    • Runs of homozygosity. Long identical stretches hint at related parents and favour recessive causes.

    Phenotype is half the search

    Clinicians describe the patient with Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms. Tools such as Exomiser combine those terms with variant rarity and predicted damage to rank genes, using databases like OMIM, Orphanet and ClinGen. Matchmaker services such as GeneMatcher connect clinicians who find the same unusual gene in different patients.

    Tools and files you will meet

    • Files: FASTQ (reads), BAM/CRAM (aligned), VCF (variants).
    • Calling: BWA-MEM2, GATK, DeepVariant.
    • Annotation: VEP, ANNOVAR.
    • Frequency: gnomAD.
    • Prediction: REVEL, CADD, SpliceAI, AlphaMissense.
    • Evidence: ClinVar, ClinGen, DECIPHER.

    Consent, privacy and secondary findings

    Exome data can reveal things nobody asked about. Many labs offer to report secondary findings in a short list of actionable genes (the ACMG list, around 80 genes, for example hereditary cancer and heart conditions), but only with consent. Genetic counselling before and after testing covers what a result can and cannot say for relatives.
