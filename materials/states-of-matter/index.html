Solids, liquids and gases are made of the same particles. What changes is how fast they move and how tightly they hold on to each other.

States of matter

Every particle here pulls on its neighbours when they are close and pushes back when they touch. At low temperature they lock into a crystal (solid). With more energy they slide past each other but stay together (liquid). With more still they fly apart and fill the box (gas). Nothing here is drawn by hand: the behaviour comes from computing the forces between every pair of particles many times a second.

What is simplified

This is a model, a small molecular dynamics simulation of 108 particles with the Lennard-Jones force, the standard simple model of atoms such as argon. Temperatures are in the model's own units, not degrees. A light pull of gravity keeps the liquid at the bottom, and a thermostat holds the temperature you set (except in step 4, where heat is added at a steady rate). With so few particles the changes are less sharp than in a real substance, and the state shown on the card is estimated from the average number of close neighbours each particle has.

Sources

Allen and Tildesley, Computer Simulation of Liquids (the Lennard-Jones model and velocity-Verlet method). Melting points of ice (0 °C) and iron (~1,538 °C): CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics.
