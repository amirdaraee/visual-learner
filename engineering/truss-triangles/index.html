Why bridges use triangles

A frame of bars joined by pins that turn freely is only stiff if it is made of triangles. A square can lean into a parallelogram without any bar changing length, so it folds; a triangle cannot. In a triangulated frame, a truss, every bar is simply pulled (tension) or pushed (compression) along its length.

What is simplified

The forces on this page are calculated, not drawn by hand: each frame is solved with the direct stiffness method, the standard method for pin-jointed frames, assuming straight steel bars with a cross-section of about 20 cm² (E·A ≈ 400,000 kN), perfect pin joints and no weight of their own. The bridge spans 8.4 m with six panels 1.3 m deep, pinned at one end and on rollers at the other; the truck weighs 50 kN (about 5 tonnes), shared equally by the two trusses and passed to the nearest joints. The sag is drawn 100 times larger than computed. Real bridges also carry their own weight, have stiffer joints, and bars can buckle long before they break.
