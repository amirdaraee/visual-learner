Why we have seasons

Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.4° and keeps pointing the same way in space. Around June the northern half leans toward the Sun: the noon Sun is high, its light is concentrated, and the days are long. Around December it is the southern half's turn. Earth's distance from the Sun changes by only about 3 % and is not the cause.

What is simplified

The model is schematic: Earth and the Sun are far too big for the size of the orbit. The orbit itself is drawn with its true, nearly circular shape, but Earth moves round it at an even pace, while the real Earth goes slightly faster near the Sun. Dates of the solstices, equinoxes and closest approach move by about a day from year to year. Noon sunlight is the share of an overhead Sun's light that reaches flat ground, before the air absorbs any. Day lengths use the tilt alone; the bending of sunlight by the air and the width of the Sun's disc add a few minutes. Real temperatures also lag behind the sunlight by weeks and depend on oceans, winds and height, which are not modelled.
